Measured Notes on AI Upscaling

Fourteen guides where every number came from ffmpeg and numpy on real files. No vendor benchmarks, no preset-name arguments, and every claim has a reproduction snippet. Where a measurement contradicted the hypothesis, the contradiction is what got published.

The measurement that organises the rest

Interpolation methods ranked by PSNR are ranked almost exactly backwards by detail retention. On a 4× upscale of a Siemens-star test pattern:

MethodPSNRHF detail retained
nearest24.60 dB25.4%
bilinear25.68 dB3.9%
bicubic26.27 dB2.1%
lanczos26.47 dB1.3%

At 2× instead of 4×, Lanczos retained 21.5% of the HF ratio against 1.3% at 4× on the 400×400 test pattern this table came from. On a second, larger 36-spoke pattern the same experiment gives 38.0% against 14.4% — the same direction, but a factor of 2.6 rather than 16. The "16×" is not reproducible across source designs, and the percentage ratio was never the right way to say it anyway — see the scale-factor test below, which compares both effects in dB and puts the real advantage at 3.0×. The qualitative point survives: which scale factor you pick matters more than which kernel you pick. The size of that margin does not.

The textbook answer is that resampling must happen in linear light. Do the four metrics in this cluster agree with each other?

Every guide here scores its experiments with some subset of four numbers: PSNR, HF retention, phase coherence, and ink coverage. This section asks the question those guides individually could not: if you rank four resampling kernels by each of those metrics, do you get the same answer? The answer is no — and the disagreement is systematic, not noise.

Design: four synthetic sources at 512×512 with deliberately different frequency structure, downsampled to 128×128 with an area filter, then brought back to 512×512 with ffmpeg at flags=neighbor|bilinear|bicubic|lanczos. PSNR and phase coherence are largely the same measurement wearing two names. HF retention measures something else.

The cost of trusting the wrong metric

Regret = how many dB below the PSNR-optimal kernel you land if you follow a metric's winner. Following HF retention costs a mean of 1.79 dB and up to 4.79 dB on the Siemens star; following ink coverage costs a mean of 1.88 dB, the same worst case.

Metric followedMean regretWorst caseDistinct winners / 4
PSNR0.000.003
HF retention1.794.791
Phase coherence0.190.442
Ink coverage1.884.792

The last column is the diagnostic that matters. HF retention picked nearest for all four content classes — one answer regardless of input. A metric whose winner never changes is not measuring your content, it is measuring its own bias. PSNR picked three different winners across the same four sources.

Why HF retention picks the worst kernel

On the Siemens star, nearest scores 148.7% HF retention — it holds 49% more high-frequency energy than the ground truth. That is not recovered detail; it is block structure. The star's spokes are near-Nyquist at the centre, and pixel replication turns them into hard-edged squares that read as broadband energy to an FFT. The same kernel scores 19.77 dB PSNR and 0.024 phase coherence on the same image, both the worst of the four.

On the line-art source the effect inverts in magnitude: nearest reaches only 59.7% HF retention while still winning the HF ranking, because every other kernel smooths the thin 2-pixel strokes away and leaves nothing for the ratio to count. So HF retention votes for nearest both when it overshoots by 49% and when it undershoots by 40%. The metric is not sign-aware.

What to do with this. Never compare kernels across two different metrics and treat agreement as confirmation — here the two most-trusted metrics disagree on 75% of pairs. Pick the metric that encodes the failure you actually care about: PSNR or phase coherence if you want the image to match the source, ink coverage if thin strokes must survive, and HF retention only as a sign-blind hint, never as a tiebreaker.

Reproduce it

# 4 sources at 512², down 4x with area, up 4x with each kernel
for k in neighbor bilinear bicubic lanczos; do
  ffmpeg -y -v error -i gt.png -vf scale=128:128:flags=area small.png
  ffmpeg -y -v error -i small.png -vf scale=512:512:flags=$k out_$k.png
done

Scale factor versus kernel, compared in dB

The HF percentages above are a ratio of two ratios, which is why they swing so widely between source designs. Read along a row: what scale factor costs. Read down a column: what kernel choice buys. Averaged over the three sources, the full kernel ladder from nearest to lanczos is worth 2.83 dB at any fixed scale. Moving the scale factor from 8× to 2× is worth 8.45 dB. So the scale decision is worth 3.0× the entire kernel decision — the same conclusion as the HF table, but stated in a unit where the two effects are actually commensurable.

The ratio is itself content-dependent, and the ordering of the three sources is the informative part. On the Siemens star, which has near-Nyquist content everywhere, kernel choice still buys 5.21 dB — a real decision. On broadband random texture, which is already band-limited by construction, kernel choice buys only 1.27 dB, while scale still buys 6.95 dB. The more near-Nyquist detail a source has, the more the kernel matters. That is the condition the "16×" number left out.

Method notes

  • HF retention — ratio of 2-D FFT magnitude outside a radius of 0.18 × image width to the magnitude inside it. Compare methods on the same image, never across images.
  • PSNR — ffmpeg psnr filter, both inputs forced to the same size with a bicubic scale so the comparison is per-pixel.
  • Phase coherence — normalized cross-correlation of the high-frequency Fourier components against ground truth. Separates real detail from sharpened artifacts, which HF energy alone cannot do.
  • Ink coverage — fraction of pixels below mid-grey (128). Appropriate for binary content, meaningless for photographs.
